# The Webinar Playbook — synthesis of Heik (2026 funnel) + Jason Fladlien (One to Many + trainings)

Reference doc for the Rauf webinar build. Everything below is distilled from the three source
trainings. Where the two sources disagree, the disagreement is called out explicitly and a ruling
is made for our use case (high-ticket, live, pitch = book a call).

---

## 0. What a webinar actually is

A webinar is not a class. It is **a long-form advertisement that pays its own way in value.**

Why it works (Fladlien):
- **Compresses the sales cycle.** What takes 7–10 days of touchpoints takes 90–120 minutes.
- **Educates and sells in one sitting.** Purchases happen emotionally, and emotion is felt in the
  moment. You create the belief and monetise it before the feeling decays.
- **It is an event.** iTunes vs. a concert.
- **It attracts the vital few.** Only the most motivated slice of a market will give you two hours.
  Fladlien: "webinars are a magnet that attract the best buyers in your market" — the people dying
  of thirst.

Heik's 2026 framing of the same thing: trust is at an all-time low, the number of touchpoints and
the *minutes of content consumed* before someone buys has gone up. A webinar delivers 90–120
minutes of consumption in one hit, so a sales cycle that would take weeks of Reels and YouTube
collapses into days.

**Core consequence for us:** every design decision is judged against "does this move belief?", not
"is this good teaching?" You can give the best information the audience has ever heard and sell
nothing (Fladlien has done it). Education for education's sake does not convert.

## 1. The architecture

Both sources land on the same skeleton. Heik's 5 sections is the frame we will use:

| Section | Job | Time (of a 2h webinar) |
|---|---|---|
| **Pre-roll / waiting room** | Live proof, chat warm-up, first soft yeses | 5–10 min |
| **1. Introduction** | Hook, authority, sell the mechanism, commitment, open loops, first pass at objections | 20–30 min |
| **2. Content** | 3–5 points. Demonstrate the mechanism works, create buy-in and "I need help" | 50–60 min |
| **3. Transition** | Recap → yes-ladder → two choices → permission to pitch | 5–8 min |
| **4. Pitch** | Core offer (short) → bonuses (long) → guarantee → CTA | 20–30 min |
| **5. Q&A** | Scripted objection destruction disguised as Q&A, then open chat | 30–45 min |

Fladlien's 14 steps map onto this exactly: hook, pain, tease, excite, position, paradigm (=1);
mechanisms, commitment (=2); transition (=3); offer, price, bonuses, risk, scarcity (=4).

His other framing — **hook → pain → excite → position → shift → transition → close** — is the same
spine with the *shift* named as its own step. The shift is the highest-leverage idea in either
training and is discussed in §3.

### Write it in this order (not presentation order)
1. **Content** first — it defines everything else.
2. **Pitch** second — write it while the content is fresh so you can plant setups backwards into it.
3. **Transition** third — it's short and formulaic.
4. **Introduction last** — you cannot introduce something that doesn't exist yet. Open loops are
   written after the content exists, because each loop is a promise you now know you can pay off.

Fladlien's staging: **shippable → polish → recalibrate.** The worst webinar that's in front of a
customer beats the best one that isn't. Real optimisation only happens after real people with real
money have seen it. (His WP Twin webinar did $5k, he fixed one confusing section, then did $100k+
to *the same audience*.)

## 2. The Introduction

Five objectives (Fladlien), which Heik's structure delivers in this order: hook → authority →
sell the mechanism → commitments → open loops, with objections sprinkled throughout.

### 2.1 The hook
The hook must do two things: **get attention AND increase desire of ownership.** "Free beer" gets
attention and then disappoints. Every part of the funnel — ads, reg page, emails, intro — is
downstream of the hook, so it gets written first at the concept level.

The mechanism of a good hook is a **pattern interrupt.** The audience arrives with pre-built
negative beliefs about (a) you, (b) webinars, (c) your topic. Break the frame in the first 60
seconds or they mentally file you as "another one of those."

Hook types available to us:
- **Live proof** (Heik's favourite, and mine for Rauf). Log into the real dashboard/account on
  screen while people are still filing in. Refresh it live. Then immediately connect it to them:
  "you're here because X — put a yes in the chat if that's you." Proof + interaction + first
  micro-commitment inside 90 seconds.
- **Contrarian / belief inversion.** State the market's consensus belief, then invert it. ("Everyone
  says AI made ecom easier. AI has made it nearly impossible to build a profitable ecom business in
  2026 — and here's why they're right, which is exactly the problem.") Steelman their belief first,
  concede it, then show why the concession is the trap.
- **Pop quiz / true-false** (Fladlien). 5 true-or-false questions, each of which secretly kills an
  objection. Creates interaction immediately and is unlike any webinar they've attended.
- **Fortune teller.** "I'll pretend to be a fortune teller and read your next 12 months regarding
  [problem]. Tell me how accurate I am."
- **Two agendas** (Fladlien's original). "I have two agendas: give you the best free solution
  you've ever seen on X, and sell you something. If I fail at the first, do not buy. If I succeed,
  promise you'll seriously consider the offer. Deal?" — pre-frames the pitch and makes the
  transition trivially easy later.
- Big promise, story, bold claim.

### 2.2 Pain before gain
Fladlien's biggest single contribution: **wake them from the nightmare before you sell the dream.
Provide the path out of hell before the path to heaven.** Nobody goes to therapy because life is
great. People attend webinars because they're suffering.

Practical method: spend an hour writing down **every excuse, big and small,** that would stop
someone doing what you teach. Then *say them out loud* in the first 3–5 minutes. You do not have to
answer them yet. Naming them does three things:
1. Shows (not tells) that you understand them — "this person gets me."
2. Deflates them. An excuse spoken aloud is smaller than the movie playing in their head.
3. Opens a loop — they now watch the whole webinar hunting for your solution to *their* excuse.

Most presenters hope the audience forgets its excuses. They never forget. They sleep with them.

### 2.3 Excite / hope for the future
The contrast to the pain. Two reliable tools:
- **The why / miracle formula.** Get them to type *their* why into the chat, with sensory
  specificity ("what will your face look like when you succeed? who will be cheering you on? who
  else is better off?"). Their why + your what and how = the miracle. The why is the single greatest
  motivator that exists, and most webinars skip it entirely.
- **Time machine.** Take them forward to the point where they've mastered this, make them look
  around at that life, then bring them back and link today's content to that future.

### 2.4 Position (authority)
Two positions must be taken (Fladlien): **you** — uniquely qualified, they solve this through you or
they don't solve it; and **the opportunity** — why now, why the normal way fails, why your approach
is the difference between winning and losing.

Four levers: **results, positioning, celebrity, testimonials.**

Rules that both sources agree on:
- **Show, don't tell.** Real dashboards, real screenshots, real text messages — not a slide with a
  typed number. Fladlien shows the actual affiliate leaderboard and a real SMS from his partner.
- **Three tiers of result, always:** the *historical monster* result (money shot), the *recent*
  result (proves it still works — "if it's not new it's not good" is a universal objection), and the
  *relatable / average-extraordinary* result. A $200M claim goes blank in a stranger's head. Six
  results of $100–200k are believable and aspirational.
- **Quantity beats magnitude.** Given a webinar with 2 giant results vs one with 100 small results,
  Fladlien takes the 100 small ones every time. "Your webinar should be dripping with proof."
- **Relatable but aspirational.** Show the come-up story (relatable) *and* the room they want to be
  in (aspirational). Then-and-now positioning: "I was recently where you are."
- **Celebrity by proxy** works even when unrelated to the topic. Use like salt.
- **Five testimonials in the first five minutes** — but never a slide-flip parade of screenshots.
  Build context around each. **Slice one long testimonial into three parts and use them in the
  intro, the content, and the close.** A testimonial used three times stops being a testimonial and
  becomes a story.
- Best proof of all is being interesting — showing them you've thought about their problem more
  than they have.

### 2.5 Commitments
Get a **mega-commitment in writing in the first five minutes.** Not a nod, not a thought — typed
into the chat.

Heik's version (logical, non-corny, and the one to model for Rauf):
> "Everyone you just saw paid me thousands of dollars to learn what I'm about to share with you for
> free. It's a well-known principle that when people pay, they pay attention. So here's the deal:
> pretend you just paid $5,000 for this free training, because the insights are worth at least that
> if you actually use them. Fair enough? Put a yes in the chat. Good — phone away, notebook out.
> I'm going to show you how this model works step by step, then at the end I'll make you an offer
> to help you implement it. Sound good?"

Note what happened: the pitch is now *pre-authorised*. They said yes to being pitched, in writing,
25 minutes before the pitch.

Fladlien's version is the same move dressed as an agenda: "I'll give you my A-level best; in return
I ask you to see yourself doing what I show you. Thoughts become things. Is it okay to move forward
with this agreement in place? Prove it — a yes in the chat right now."

**Why it works:** commitments create identity, and identity demands congruence. If you've agreed
you'll give your best, then later not investing is incongruent — and incongruence hurts.

Soft commitments start even earlier, during the sound check: "give me a yes without using the word
yes" and read the creative answers aloud. You're teaching the room that saying yes to you is fun.

### 2.6 Open loops / mystery
Information-gap theory: the brain feels tension between what it knows and what it wants to know.
That tension is what keeps people on for two hours.

Method (Fladlien's "first, last"): put a slide in the intro that says "On this webinar…[fill later]",
finish the whole webinar, then come back and write **blind bullets** — promise the benefit, hide the
mechanism. Heik uses ~5 slides of these. Nest them, and close them out of order — the randomness
raises attentiveness.

You can also foreshadow the *offer itself* here (show a corner of the workbook / the bonus tool).
Recognition enhances perceived value: when they see it again in the pitch it feels more valuable.

## 3. The Content

### 3.1 The one rule
**Heik: teach the WHAT, not the HOW. Fladlien: one great insight beats ten decent ones; less is
more.** Same rule.

Teach too little → no expertise → no sale. Teach too much → confusion → no sale. A confused mind
doesn't say no, it says nothing.

The target state at the end of content:
1. It makes sense and it works. (belief)
2. I can see myself doing it. (ownership)
3. It takes real effort and doing it alone will be messy. (the gap you monetise)

Fladlien's version of #3 is blunter and better: get them to "I am absolutely committed to doing
this," so the only question left is **"do I do it alone with time, or with you with money?"**

Fladlien's early mistake is the canonical warning: he taught **four** traffic methods to
"over-deliver." Wrong. They want relief, not options. One clear path.

### 3.2 Structure: 3–5 points, never more, never fewer
Both sources: no fewer than three, no more than five. Fewer means you merged steps; more means your
outcome is too ambitious or your steps are too granular.

The points must flow with obvious logic (idea → validate → build → market). Show the roadmap on a
slide and **tick off each step as you finish it** — kills confusion, creates an open loop, and makes
the whole thing look small enough to attempt ("it's only four steps").

Inside each point you use either **steps-within-the-step** or **criteria** ("here are the 5 factors
that make X work"). Pick by feel; switch if it lands flat.

### 3.3 The unit of content: CONTEXT → VISION → STRATEGY → COMMITMENT

This is Fladlien's chapter 7 and it's the single most transferable piece of craft in the book. Heik
teaches the same loop in shorter form (context/re-hook → teach → micro-commitment → transition).

**CONTEXT** — before you show anything, answer: why is this step crucial, and what's involved? This
is a *re-hook* for every point. Surface the questions they're privately asking ("I don't have an
idea yet… I have too many ideas… what if it's saturated?") and promise clarity by the end of the
section. Buy-in leads to buying: "until you see the promise, you won't endure the pain."

**VISION** — a slice-of-life that they can step into. Either (a) a day in *their* future life using
this, with sensory detail and small concrete numbers, (b) a micro-story from your own past that they
can inhabit, or (c) a customer's micro-story. Fladlien's Raul story does double duty: it paints the
vision AND destroys the money objection AND supplies proof, all in one narrative.

**STRATEGY** — the actual teaching. High level. Show tools and outputs, not setup and clicks. Spend
~30–35% of content time on point one: if you lose them on step one you lose them for good, and
pacing should start slow and accelerate into the pitch.

**COMMITMENT** — close every point with a yes. Then transition to the next point *as if they have
already done this one* ("now that you have your winning idea, we need to build it") — this creates
ownership.

### 3.4 Persuasional foreplay (the setup mechanic)
The content section is where you build the demand your bonuses will satisfy.

- Show how critical something is, and how painful/expensive/slow it is to do manually → then hand it
  over free as a bonus in the pitch. (Fladlien: teaches 16 hours of keyword research, gets
  commitment that it's worth 16 hours, then reveals the tool that does it in 16 minutes. Heik:
  shows how vital influencers are and how hard they are to reach, then gifts the $500/mo tool.)
- **Deliberately create an objection you already have the answer to.** Fladlien shows an expensive
  tool knowing the audience thinks "I don't have that" — then gives it away.
- Kill objections *inside* the teaching, ideally with a testimonial that handles three at once
  rather than a slide that says "objection #1."
- Drop a testimonial immediately after any teaching point that would trigger doubt.

### 3.5 Micro-commitments
Heik: get 20 yeses before the pitch. Fladlien: 150. Either way, by the time you ask for the real
yes it is the most natural thing in the world.

Commitment phrases, positioned at the start, middle, and end of sentences so they don't get
noticed: *right? makes sense? isn't it? doesn't it? agreed? correct? see what I mean? got it?*

Hard vs soft: "Does that make sense?" (hard, checks in) vs "That makes sense, doesn't it?" (soft,
assumes). Mostly hard — it keeps you talking *to* the room instead of *at* it. And if the room
doesn't get it, say so and re-explain; don't pretend.

The **1-2-3 commitment formula**: broad question → specific question → big question.
> "Did you enjoy our time so far?" → "Would you like to spend more time than just this webinar?" →
> "If I had something that takes what we did today to the next level, would you want to know about
> it?"

Never be lazy about it. "Who here wants more money?" is hack. "I'm going to assume you want to get
unreasonably wealthy in an unreasonable amount of time — is my assumption correct?" does the same
job without insulting anyone.

### 3.6 Dimensionalisation
For any point that carries real logical weight, **make one big point three ways** rather than three
points one way each. Same insight, three data sets, three angles, delivered differently so it isn't
monotonous. This is how you get "oh, I get it now" instead of "sure, okay."

### 3.7 Emotional management (ecological checks)
While editing content, run every section against: what emotional state does this create? Does it
create confidence or confusion? Does it make the fear of *inaction* larger than the fear of trying?
Does it isolate them or connect them?

The emotions that matter: **fear** (costs more sales than anything — associate it with inaction),
**confusion** (kill with roadmapping), **enthusiasm** (contagious from your voice), **safety**,
**anger** (careful; the safe version is getting them fired up to prove *you* wrong), **loneliness**.

## 4. The Shift (the highest-leverage idea in either training)

Fladlien's core question, which he calls the multi-million dollar question:

> **"What isn't this audience hearing that, if they did hear it, would cause them to buy?"**

Everyone in your market has 40 other people saying the same things in slightly different words.
Those competitors move the prospect down the field to the 1-yard line and never score — and a deal
that is 99% closed is worth exactly zero. Your job is to say the one thing nobody else is saying,
push them over the line, do 5% of the work and take 100% of the money.

The thing that blocks the sale is almost always **a limit they place on themselves.** If they see
themselves as a failure, you will never sell them on being a success until you remove the limit.
You have to shatter the limiting belief so thoroughly that using the old excuse would feel stupid.

Two shift patterns worth stealing:

**Smaller results sooner > larger results later.** Nobody has experienced a million dollars; everyone
has experienced ten. And a long timeline is a long window in which to screw it up and quit. Shrink
the timeframe and you delete the "I'll give up before it works" excuse. The elegant part: half the
audience buys in agreement ("I can see myself doing that"), and half buys in *disagreement* ("screw
you, I'm going big"). Both buy.

**Change the circumstances, not the person.** You can get better at poker, or you can find a table
full of bad players betting big. Getting better is hard; finding the table is easy. Every strong
webinar asks: *what is the least amount of change I can require from this audience for them to solve
their problem?*

For Rauf, the time-based ranges strategy is our **paradigm/shift candidate** — the "oh, this changes
everything" moment. The test it must pass: after hearing it, does the prospect think *what I thought
was a limitation is actually an advantage*, or *the way everyone told me to do this is exactly why
it hasn't worked*? If it doesn't produce that reaction, the mechanism is under-framed, not weak.

## 5. The Transition

Shortest section, hugely underrated, almost nobody plans it. Its job is to get the audience into the
most receptive state to hear an offer — and to get *you* into the right state to make one. Punchline
vs setup: the state you put someone in immediately before the offer matters more than the offer.

**The four beats:**

1. **Recap — "60 minutes in 60 seconds."** Slide title: "So far on this training we've covered…"
   ("so far" implies more). Then fire 10–20 of your best points in rapid succession, phrased as
   things they now *possess*, with rising energy. It reminds them consciously of value they've
   already forgotten, and it puts you in the right mood to sell ("look what I just did for these
   people without asking for anything").

2. **The yes-ladder — half a dozen yeses.** "Knowing what you now know… do you see the opportunity?
   Can you see yourself doing this? Was your time here well spent? Would you like to go deeper than
   one webinar?" That last question is the bridge — the only way to go deeper is to buy.

3. **The two choices (crossroads close).** The most forklifted piece of webinar copy in existence —
   Bob Proctor and Alex Hormozi both used it near-verbatim.
   > "Before doing this today, I was faced with one of two choices. Option one: share what I've
   > shared, part ways, and hope you can figure the rest out on your own. And if that were your only
   > option, it would be a worthy ideal to pursue. But it isn't. Option two: I could take a more
   > active role and responsibility for your success — arm you with every resource I have, stack the
   > deck in your favour, and make success all but inevitable. I chose the second option, and when
   > you see it, you'll be glad I did."

   Heik's upgrade for cold traffic: **between the two options, enumerate the specific problems they
   will hit if they go alone** (can't find a developer, waste months building the wrong thing, get
   burned overseas). That converts "worthy ideal" into "expensive mistake" without you badmouthing
   anybody.

   Technically this is a false dilemma — there are more than two options — and that's the point: it
   collapses the decision to black and white and positions the offer as a *favour*.

4. **Ask permission to pitch.** "Which option makes more sense — one or two? Drop it in the chat."
   (Everyone types 2.) "Do you mind if I take a couple of minutes to show you what I built that does
   exactly that?" (Everyone types yes.) They have now asked to be sold to, in writing.

## 6. The Pitch

### 6.1 Offer architecture: core / bonuses / guarantee
Restructure whatever the existing offer is into three buckets before writing a word.

**Rule for sorting:** if it's obvious and expected → core. If it has a wow factor and would surprise
them → bonus. If it's "thin" (huge benefit, quick to consume) → bonus.

**Core offer: 3–5 minutes. That's it.** Both sources are emphatic. The core offer is not what makes
someone buy on a live webinar — it puts them in **maybe**. Presenters who lovingly detail every
module bore the room, and it's hard to bore someone into buying.

Core offer flow (Fladlien):
- Product name + tagline
- One-sentence benefit statement (pack 4–5 benefits into it)
- Major components — for each: **sexy headline → 3–4 feature+benefit bullets → future pace → proof**

Name it low-key if the market is hyped; hype it if the market is flat. Progress from subtle (name) →
less subtle (tagline) → not subtle at all (benefit statement).

### 6.2 Bonuses are the sale
You will spend **2–3× longer on bonuses than on the core offer.** Hormozi's $100M Offers launch deck:
core offer around slide 500–600, and roughly a thousand slides after that are bonuses.

Rules:
- **3 minimum, 5–7 ideal.** Fladlien likes seven.
- **Second-best bonus first. Best bonus last.** Tease from the start that you've saved the best for
  last. The last bonus is the knockout punch delivered when they're already on the edge — and it
  lands right before you drop the application link.
- **Every bonus should do at least one of three things:** kill an objection, be a dramatic
  demonstration of value, or carry a proof element. Objection-killing bonuses are the highest ROI —
  map each of your top objections to a bonus that annihilates it.
- **Build context around every bonus.** Never "and bonus two is X." Explain why it exists, why it's
  valuable, why they're getting it. Give it a retail value and anchor it.
- **Justify why it's a bonus and why now** — real constraint ("the dev team only takes 10 people a
  month"), not invented urgency.
- **Compound after each bonus:** re-list everything they're getting so far, phrased slightly
  differently each time. Reveal → review → reveal → review.
- **Call to action after every single bonus.** Fladlien asks for the order 26 times per webinar; the
  average campaign asks 4.
- Bonuses can be third-party (borrow someone's course for the exposure), or **conditional** ("book a
  call with me once you've done $10k") — conditional bonuses let you offer something outrageous and
  double as a testimonial-generation machine.
- Make them **exclusive** — unavailable anywhere else, at any price. Exclusivity is scarcity.

### 6.3 Price
For a direct-buy webinar: reveal price **early** (within ~5 minutes of starting the offer), because
until they know the number they can't evaluate anything, and because most people say no the first
time — get the no out of the system early so every subsequent bonus is a fresh chance at yes.

**Mullet technique: offer and price short in the front, bonuses and value long in the back.**

Anchor by stair-stepping down: what it's worth → what others pay for comparable → what I normally
charge → today's number. Cheesy, and it out-converts not doing it every time Fladlien has tested.
Price is meaningless in isolation; it only means something relative to what preceded it (the $5,000
suit makes the $1,000 suit cheap; the $100 suit makes it expensive).

**Our case (high ticket, book-a-call):** Heik's ruling — you do not have to drop the price on the
webinar if a closer is going to handle it. The "price" beat is replaced by the application/Typeform
CTA. But the *anchoring logic* still applies to the bonus stack: each bonus gets a retail value so
the perceived value of the package is enormous before the call ever happens. And the call itself is
positioned as the next logical step, not a sales call.

### 6.4 Guarantee / risk reversal
People buy when the result is big enough; people *definitely* buy when the risk is gone.

- A guarantee should always exist in some form, even conditional or abstract.
- **Better-than-money-back is the rarest and strongest tool available** (<0.1% of webinars use one).
  Structure: ask for a small, trackable behavioural commitment; if they do it and don't get the
  result, they get more than their money back. This does two things — it converts, and it makes
  customers *succeed*, because the condition is the behaviour that causes success.
- Heik's high-ticket version: "if you do X, Y, Z and still don't make your investment back, I work
  with you for free until you're ROI positive."
- **Risk arbitrage:** take a risk that is real to them but not to you.
- Construct it like an actuary — feasible to claim, unlikely to be claimed en masse (most buyers
  never start, quit early, or don't implement). Fladlien's profit guarantee cost $180k in payouts
  against ~$20M in sales.
- **Always give the reason why** you're offering a guarantee this strong, or you create the
  objection "why is he trying so hard?"
- Never fake it, never make conditions unobtainable — the market smells it.

### 6.5 Scarcity
Last of the 14 steps, but sprinkled throughout. Must be **true**, must have a **reason why**, and the
reason should ideally be outside your control ("the vendor would only license 100 seats"). Cohort
start dates are the cleanest legitimate scarcity: the bus leaves on the 14th, we can't wait around.

Without urgency, the default decision is no decision.

## 7. The Q&A (where the money actually lands)

Heik: **the Q&A is a scripted objection-destruction session disguised as a Q&A**, and the majority of
applications come in during it. Stay on 30–45 minutes minimum after the CTA. Fladlien: he has clients
whose webinar is 4 hours — 1 hour of presentation and 3 hours of reasons to buy, each one read
word-for-word off a script.

Structure:
1. Open: "I can see some of you have already applied, which is awesome. For those still here, I know
   you've got a question or two you want answered before you decide — let's handle those now."
2. Run your **scripted** objections first, as slides. They cluster into three buckets: **money,
   time, product-specific.** After each one, another CTA.
3. Then open the chat and answer live for as long as people keep asking. Applications keep flowing
   the whole time.

**The governing principle: new insight → new decision.** Anyone still on the call is the right fit
and hasn't heard the one reason that unlocks them yet. Zoom doesn't charge by the minute and
PowerPoint doesn't charge by the slide. His reason may differ from her reason; some people need
three reasons. You lose nothing by giving more — the only thing stopping you is not knowing enough
of them.

**Corollary: the reason most people quit early is a lack of nerve, not a lack of interest.** Most
people who say no stay on the webinar. Saying no is how some people give themselves permission to
say yes later on their own terms.

### The close library (memorise these; adapt to Rauf)
- **Two choices / crossroads** — see §5.
- **Old habits die hard** — one webinar won't change habits you've had for years; you need repeated
  exposure, resources, and accountability, and I'm not going to let you drift back.
- **Nothing special** — I'm of average intelligence with no advantages; I just spent years failing.
  You could do the same. Why would you, when you can buy the shortcut?
- **Results or excuses** — you can be good at one, never both. Pick.
- **Information alone isn't enough** — imagine a teacher dropping a textbook on your desk and coming
  back at the end of term. You need tools, community, accountability, coaching.
- **What money is good for** — money has no value except what you exchange it for.
- **It beats working at McDonald's** — reduce the offer to a per-hour saving vs your time.
- **The pricing decision** — we could have built the cheap version everyone builds, or asked "what
  would it take to make success inevitable?" We chose the second. It turns off most of the market.
  Good.
- **Do nothing or…** — how's the current approach working out?
- **Hand-hold close** — literally walk them through the page, the button, the form, the confirmation
  email. Removes every micro-friction.
- **Fully informed decision** — I'm not asking you to decide yes or no; I'm asking you to make a
  fully informed decision, and you can only do that from the inside. You don't buy a house without
  seeing the inside.
- **Footprints** — you get the solution, and you get me.
- **93 million miles** — state something enormous immediately before asking for something small.
  (Cialdini: any large number preceding the ask makes the ask feel smaller.)

Fladlien's "dead sleep" test: you're only good at this when someone could wake you at 3am and you
could rattle off five money closes and five time closes cold. He built that by memorising one Zig
Ziglar close a day for 30 days. Rote memorisation is usually useless; closes are the exception.

## 8. Craft rules (apply everywhere)

1. **Show, don't tell.** Real artefacts over typed claims, every time.
2. **One idea per slide.** Break long bullets across many slides. Write words-only first, cut on the
   second pass, add imagery on the third. Never design while writing.
3. **Slides beat video production** nine times out of ten for conversion. Production competes with
   the message; slides make the abstract concrete. (Listeners retain ~50% immediately and ~25% after
   two months — help them.)
4. **Defocus everything except the thing you want them to look at.** Blur the screenshot, red-box
   the number.
5. **Cut ~25% of your content on the second pass**, and more after each live run.
6. **Keep the CTA URL on every slide from the first CTA onward.** Build a master slide with a CTA
   bar. Fladlien has the URL on ~two-thirds of post-pitch slides. Make the URL trivially easy to say,
   spell, and type.
7. **Advance some slides faster than they can write.** Forces attention, gently instils loss.
8. **Continuity between sections** — reuse the same example product/person/case across steps so each
   reference compounds.
9. **Repeat key paradigm points**, engagingly, in different clothes.
10. **Humour belongs in the intro**, to put them at ease before you demolish the objections later.
11. **Challenge the audience.** Pure pandering underperforms. Fladlien's "does that mean you can do
    it? …probably not, actually" is a deliberate pattern interrupt that creates the tension his offer
    resolves.
12. **Never end the webinar the moment you pitch.** The longer you stay, the more you sell — and it's
    the most legible proof that you care.

## 9. Objections: the systematic treatment

**Universal objections** (assume all of these until proven otherwise): money, time, belief ("I've
failed before"), ability, trust ("too good to be true"), fear (of change/failure/embarrassment),
conflict (solving this betrays who I am), confusion (where do I start?), timing (too early / missed
the boat).

**Product-specific objections** come from market research — Heik's method: feed 25–50 sales call
transcripts + your VSL + your YouTube content into an LLM with a market research prompt, and mine the
output for the exact language of objections, personas, and desired outcomes. This document is the
source of truth for ads, emails, content, bonuses and Q&A. Every objection in it must be dead before
the pitch.

**Three ways to treat any objection:**
- **Ignore** — only ever for small ones, deliberately.
- **Disagree** — the standard. Formula: *acknowledge & soften → validate & reframe → present a
  target.* ("You may be wondering whether you can do this. That's a valid concern, because what you
  really want to know is: will this time be different? As we go through today, watch how this works
  for people from wildly different backgrounds — look for that, and it'll answer itself.") Soft
  words: *wondering*, *may be*, *might be on your mind*. Never *worry*, *can't*.
- **Agree** — the strongest form of validation, and it bypasses resistance. Formula: *agree → define
  what you're agreeing to → prove it → give a target.* ("These techniques don't work in every
  market. They only work in markets where the person you're selling to is a human being.")

**Include by excluding:** you can agree with an objection in a way that repels a slice of the
audience who was never going to buy, and thereby magnetise the ones who were.

**Timing:** weaken the foundation in the intro, chip at them all through the content (ideally via
testimonials rather than lecture), demolish them in the pitch and Q&A.

**Diagnostics:** if they raise objections at the pitch you never addressed → add them. If you handled
it only in the pitch and it persists → move it earlier. If you hammer it everywhere and it persists →
wrong audience, or weak technique.

## 10. High-ticket / book-a-call adaptations (our model)

Heik's funnel is built for exactly our case, and it differs from Fladlien's direct-buy model in
these specific ways:

- **No price reveal on the webinar.** The CTA is an application (Typeform/survey) dropped in the
  chat; a closer handles price. The bonus stack still gets valued so the perceived package is huge
  before the call.
- **The offer still gets pitched in full.** Core + bonuses + guarantee. The call closes on the offer
  presented; if the webinar under-pitches, the closer has to build it from scratch.
- **The webinar's job is to eliminate objections so calls are layups.** Two hours of nurture means
  the setter and closer inherit a pre-sold prospect. Every objection alive at the call is one the
  webinar failed to kill.
- **Scarcity** shifts from cart-close to cohort start, bonus quantity limits, and call availability.
- **The Q&A is the conversion engine**, not the pitch slide.
- **Post-webinar ops matter as much as the pitch.** Setters dial in priority order: (1) attended +
  booked, (2) attended + didn't book, (3) registered + no-show. The setter's only job is to get them
  talking, build the gap between where they are and where they want to be, and position the closer
  as the bridge. Never write off no-shows.

### KPI benchmarks (Heik) — for diagnosing which section is broken
| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Ad CTR | 1–3%+ |
| CPM | $20–70 |
| Landing page conversion | 25–35% |
| Cost per registration | $4–12 |
| Show rate | 25%+ cold, up to 50% warm |
| Stayed to pitch | 70–80% |
| Booking/conversion rate on webinar | 5–15% |
| Call show rate | 65–80% |
| Call close rate | 25–35% |

Bottleneck logic: low show rate → pre-webinar nurture. Low stay-to-pitch → intro and content
(hook, open loops, pacing). Low booking → offer, bonuses, transition, Q&A length. Low call close →
objections that survived the webinar.

### Pre-webinar nurture (compresses everything downstream)
7–10 day promo. Retargeting ads (20–50 objection-handling clips), 15–20 emails (~2/day), a
Telegram/WhatsApp channel using voice notes and video for intimacy, restrained SMS (confirm, 1-day,
day-of, 5-min, and one "we're live and getting to the good part" 25 minutes in). Every touch has one
job: **shift a belief** so they arrive pre-framed. Linking to long-form YouTube in the nurture stacks
"minutes consumed" before they ever show up, which measurably lifts pitch conversion.

## 11. Where the two sources disagree — and our rulings

| Question | Heik | Fladlien | Our ruling |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reveal price on the webinar? | No, for high ticket — drop the application | Yes, early, then stack bonuses | Follow Heik. High-ticket + closer. Keep Fladlien's anchoring on bonus values. |
| Open with proof or with pain? | Live proof first | Hook then pain immediately | Live proof as the pattern interrupt, then straight into the excuse litany within 3 minutes. Proof earns the right to name their pain without it stinging. |
| How emotional should commitments be? | Prefers logical ("pretend you paid $5,000") over "picture yourself holding your phone" | Uses both, including heavy vision work | Logical for the mega-commitment; keep one strong vision beat (the why / miracle formula) because it does work Heik's version doesn't. |
| Number of bonuses | 3 min, 5 ideal | 5–7, likes 7 | 5–7, each mapped to a named objection. |
| Content depth | "What, not how" | Same, plus "one point three ways" | Both. Shallow in breadth, deep in demonstration. |
| Guarantee | Prefers performance guarantee over money-back | Better-than-money-back where possible | Performance/ROI guarantee, with a reason why. |
