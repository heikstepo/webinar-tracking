# Intro + Content — the beat sheet

Sources: Heik (2026 funnel training) and Jason Fladlien (One to Many + trainings).
Where they say the same thing in different words, it's listed once with both names.
Every beat has a job. If a beat isn't doing its job, cut it — length is never the point, belief is.

**The one sentence that governs both sections:**
Judge every beat against *"does this move belief?"* — not *"is this good teaching?"*
You can deliver the best information they've ever heard and sell nothing.

---

# PART 1 — THE INTRODUCTION (20–30 min)

Heik's order, which is the one we follow:
**hook → authority → sell the mechanism → commitments → open loops**
Objections are not a section. They get sprinkled through all five, ideally inside testimonials.

Fladlien's five intro objectives map onto the same five beats:
hook / pain / excite / position / paradigm.

---

## BEAT 0 — Pre-roll (5–10 min, before you "start")
**Job:** teach the room that saying yes to you is fun, and let proof be visible before you've claimed anything.

- Live dashboard on screen while people file in. Refresh it live.
- Sound-check commitments: *"give me a yes without using the word yes"* — read the funny ones out.
- This is where the first 5 of your 20 yeses come from.

**Rauf:** the funded-account payout dashboard is already up when people arrive. Not narrated. Just there.

---

## BEAT 1 — The hook (first 60–90 seconds)
**Job (Fladlien):** get attention AND increase desire of ownership. "Free beer" gets attention and then disappoints.
**Mechanism:** a pattern interrupt. They arrive with pre-built negative beliefs about (a) you, (b) webinars, (c) your topic. Break the frame in 60 seconds or you get filed as "another one of those."

Options: live proof · contrarian belief inversion · pop-quiz true/false · fortune teller · two agendas · big promise.

**Heik's favourite, and ours:** live proof → immediately turn it on them.
Proof + interaction + first micro-commitment inside 90 seconds.

**Rauf:** payouts on screen → *"You're here because you can pass evaluations but you can't turn it into money coming out. Yes in the chat if that's you."*

---

## BEAT 2 — Authority (immediately after the hook — NOT later)
**Job (Fladlien):** take two positions. **You** — they solve this through you or they don't solve it.
**The opportunity** — why now, why the normal way fails.

The rules both sources agree on:
- **Show, don't tell.** Real dashboards, real screenshots, real texts. Never a slide with a typed number.
- **Three tiers of result, always:**
  1. the historical monster (money shot)
  2. the recent one (proves it still works — "if it's not new it's not good" is universal)
  3. the relatable / average-extraordinary one
- **Quantity beats magnitude.** 100 small results beat 2 giant ones. "Your webinar should be dripping with proof."
- **Relatable AND aspirational.** The come-up story plus the room they want to be in. *"I was recently where you are."*
- **Five testimonials in the first five minutes** — but never a slide-flip parade. Build context around each.
- **Slice one long testimonial into three parts** and use them in intro, content and close. Used three times, it stops being a testimonial and becomes a story.
- Best proof of all: being interesting. Showing you've thought about their problem more than they have.

**Rauf:** his own P&L (monster) → a recent month (still works) → 3–5 student payouts in the $2–8k range (relatable). Then the come-up: two years, thousands of pounds of courses, still couldn't do it.

---

## BEAT 3 — Pain before gain (3–5 min)
**Job (Fladlien's single biggest contribution):** wake them from the nightmare before you sell the dream. Provide the path out of hell before the path to heaven. Nobody goes to therapy because life is great.

**Method:** write down every excuse — big and small — that would stop someone doing what you teach. Then say them out loud. You do not have to answer them yet.

Naming an excuse does three things:
1. Shows (not tells) that you understand them — *"this person gets me."*
2. Deflates it. An excuse spoken aloud is smaller than the movie playing in their head.
3. Opens a loop — they now watch the whole webinar hunting for the answer to *their* excuse.

Most presenters hope the audience forgets its excuses. They never forget. They sleep with them.

**Rauf:** the three stages — blown accounts / passed evals but can't hold it / profitable but inconsistent. Named out loud, unanswered.

---

## BEAT 4 — Sell the mechanism (the opportunity)
**Job:** they must want *this specific thing* before they'll sit through you teaching it.

Name it. Show it working. Do not explain it yet.
This is where the paradigm gets set: **why the normal way fails, and why this is different in kind, not degree.**

**Rauf:** Time Based Ranges named inside the first 3 minutes, one range drawn live, one sweep, done. Then the third-party data.

---

## BEAT 5 — Excite / hope (short)
**Job (Fladlien):** contrast to the pain. Two tools:
- **The why / miracle formula.** Get them to type *their* why into the chat with sensory specificity — "what does your face look like when this works? who's cheering?" Their why + your what and how = the miracle. The single greatest motivator that exists, and most webinars skip it.
- **Time machine.** Take them forward to having mastered this, let them look around, bring them back, link today's content to that future.

Keep it short for a trading audience — it goes corny fast.

---

## BEAT 6 — The mega-commitment (in writing, inside the first 5 minutes)
**Job (both):** pre-authorise the pitch, 25 minutes before the pitch.

Heik's version — logical, non-corny, the one to model:
> "Everyone you just saw paid me thousands to learn what I'm about to share with you for free. It's a well-known principle that when people pay, they pay attention. So here's the deal — pretend you just paid $5,000 for this training, because the insights are worth at least that if you actually use them. Fair enough? Yes in the chat. Good. Phone away, notebook out. I'm going to show you how this works step by step, then at the end I'll make you an offer to help you implement it. Sound good?"

Fladlien's version, same move dressed as an agenda:
> "I'll give you my A-level best. In return I ask you to see yourself doing what I show you. Thoughts become things. Is it okay to move forward with that agreement in place? Prove it — yes in the chat right now."

**Why it works:** commitments create identity, and identity demands congruence. Having agreed to give their best, *not* investing later is incongruent — and incongruence hurts.

---

## BEAT 7 — Open loops (~5 slides, last thing in the intro)
**Job:** information-gap theory. The brain feels tension between what it knows and what it wants to know. That tension is what holds someone for two hours.

**Method (Fladlien's "first, last"):** put an "On this webinar…" slide in the intro, finish writing the entire webinar, *then* come back and fill it with **blind bullets** — promise the benefit, hide the mechanism. Nest them. Close them **out of order** — the randomness raises attentiveness.

You can also foreshadow the offer here (a corner of the workbook, the bonus tool). Recognition enhances perceived value: when they see it again in the pitch, it feels worth more.

# PART 2 — THE CONTENT (50–60 min)

## The one rule
**Heik: teach the WHAT, not the HOW. Fladlien: one great insight beats ten decent ones.** Same rule.

Teach too little → no expertise → no sale.
Teach too much → confusion → no sale. **A confused mind doesn't say no, it says nothing.**

Fladlien's canonical mistake: he taught **four** traffic methods to over-deliver. Wrong. They want relief, not options. One clear path.

## The target state at the end of content
1. **It makes sense and it works.** (belief)
2. **I can see myself doing it.** (ownership)
3. **It takes real effort and doing it alone will be messy.** (the gap you monetise)

Fladlien's blunter version of #3: get them to *"I am absolutely committed to doing this"* — so the only question left is **"do I do it alone with time, or with you with money?"**

---

## Structure: 3–5 points. Never more, never fewer.
Fewer means you merged steps. More means your outcome is too ambitious or your steps are too granular.

The points must flow with obvious logic. Put the roadmap on a slide and **tick each step off as you finish it** — kills confusion, creates an open loop, and makes the whole thing look small enough to attempt ("it's only three steps").

Inside each point, use either **steps-within-the-step** or **criteria** ("the 5 factors that make X work"). Pick by feel, switch if it lands flat.

**Rauf: three points.** Window → Bias → Trigger.

---

## THE UNIT — run this loop for every single point
**CONTEXT → VISION → STRATEGY → COMMITMENT**
(Fladlien ch. 7. Heik's shorter version: context/re-hook → teach → micro-commitment → transition.)

### CONTEXT — a re-hook for every point
Before you show anything, answer: **why is this step crucial, and what's involved?**
Surface the questions they're privately asking. Promise clarity by the end of the section.
*Buy-in leads to buying: until you see the promise, you won't endure the pain.*

Shape:
1. Why this step decides whether you make money
2. "Maybe you're asking yourself…" — three real objections, spoken as questions
3. "After this section you'll know…" — the specific deliverable
4. A sweetener that ties forward ("and this connects to what we do in step three")
5. **"Fair enough?"** → yes in chat

### VISION — a slice of life they can step into
One of three:
- a day in *their* future using this, sensory, small concrete numbers
- a micro-story from your past they can inhabit
- a customer's micro-story

Fladlien's Raul story does triple duty — paints the vision, destroys the money objection, supplies proof, all in one narrative. Aim for that.

### STRATEGY — the actual teaching
High level. **Show tools and outputs, not setup and clicks.**
Spend **~30–35% of content time on point one** — lose them on step one and you lose them for good. Pacing starts slow and accelerates into the pitch.

### COMMITMENT — close every point with a yes
Then transition to the next point **as if they've already done this one**:
*"Now that you've got your bias, we need somewhere to actually get in."*
That assumption creates ownership.

---

## Persuasional foreplay — the setup mechanic
The content section is where you build the demand your **bonuses** will satisfy.

- Show how critical something is, and how painful/expensive/slow it is manually → then hand it over free in the pitch.
  *(Fladlien teaches 16 hours of keyword research, gets agreement that it's worth 16 hours, then reveals the tool that does it in 16 minutes. Heik shows how vital influencers are and how hard to reach, then gifts the $500/mo tool.)*
- **Deliberately create an objection you already have the answer to.**
- Kill objections *inside* the teaching — ideally with a testimonial that handles three at once, never a slide that says "Objection #1."
- Drop a testimonial immediately after any teaching point that would trigger doubt.

---

## Micro-commitments
Heik: **20 yeses** before the pitch. Fladlien: **150**. Either way, by the time you ask for the real yes it's the most natural thing in the world.

Phrases, placed at the start, middle and end of sentences so they don't get noticed:
*right? · makes sense? · isn't it? · doesn't it? · agreed? · correct? · see what I mean? · got it?*

Hard vs soft: *"Does that make sense?"* (hard, checks in) vs *"That makes sense, doesn't it?"* (soft, assumes). **Mostly hard** — it keeps you talking *to* the room instead of *at* it. And if the room doesn't get it, say so and re-explain. Don't pretend.

**The 1-2-3 formula** — broad → specific → big:
> "Did you enjoy our time so far?" → "Would you like to spend more time than just this webinar?" → "If I had something that takes what we did today to the next level, would you want to know about it?"

Never be lazy. *"Who here wants more money?"* is hack. *"I'm going to assume you want to get unreasonably wealthy in an unreasonable amount of time — is my assumption correct?"* does the same job without insulting anyone.

---

## Dimensionalisation
For any point carrying real logical weight: **make one big point three ways** rather than three points once each. Same insight, three data sets, three angles, delivered differently so it isn't monotonous.

This is how you get *"oh, I get it now"* instead of *"sure, okay."*

**This is the beat we've been under-serving.** It's also the answer to the length question — length comes from dimensionalising the points you already have, not from adding points.

---

## Emotional check (run on every section while editing)
- What emotional state does this create?
- Confidence or confusion?
- Does it make the fear of **inaction** larger than the fear of trying?
- Does it isolate them or connect them?

Emotions that matter: **fear** (costs more sales than anything — always associate it with inaction), **confusion** (kill with roadmapping), **enthusiasm** (contagious from your voice), **safety**, **anger** (careful — the safe version is getting them fired up to prove *you* wrong), **loneliness**.

# BUDGETS

| | Beats | Target |
|---|---|---|
| Intro | 7 | 20–30 min |
| Content point 1 | full unit | 30–35% of content |
| Content point 2 | full unit | ~30% |
| Content point 3 | full unit | ~35%, accelerating |

**Measured from the reference class:** ~400 spoken words per teaching point, once context, proof, objection-handling and commitments are included.
Three points × three teaching points each × ~400 = **~3,600 words** of content section. That's the number to build to.
