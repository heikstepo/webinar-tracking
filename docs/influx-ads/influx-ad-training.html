The short version

Same length, same number of lines, and "we" shows up at the same point in both. The difference is what each line does.

  1. Say it, don't explain it."It's going to be very hard for you to actually close them." 11 words. I spent 59 words reasoning about why one call isn't enough for a big contract.
  2. Make them picture it."Now… think about what's going to happen if there's barely anything there." They imagine their own profile. I described it for them.
  3. Stack the cost."And not only that…" Bad, then worse. Two plain costs beat five steps of logic.
  4. Land the point out loud."So yes… building a LinkedIn brand really is THAT important if you want to…" I never concluded. I jumped straight to "The fix is…".
  5. Agree with their excuse first."But sure… your company is doing over a million dollars a year… you're busy…" It concedes, flatters and filters in one line.
  6. Then step in and say who you are."And that's where we step in. My name's Alex…" Coming in is fine once the problem has fully landed. 1.1's TV-ad problem was coming in too early, not coming in at all.
  7. The how gets numbers.Daily. Over 20 brands. 3 million followers. 15 minutes. I wrote "the right people" 7 times across my 5 ads instead.
  8. Kill objections inside the proof."Without them ever having to write a single post themselves or step in front of a camera." Two objections, one line.
  9. Qualify on what they want. Keep the CTA plain."And want LinkedIn to be an actual reliable acquisition channel…" No clever tie-back like my "make sure they like what they find".
  10. Never reuse the last ad's body.My 2.3 middle was 2.2's middle in new words: fix, no time, we do it, hour/write/engine, book.

New since 2.3

What 3.1, 3.2, 4.1, 4.2 and 4.3 added on top of the ten above. Your newest ending is rule 24.

  1. Keep the ad group's job in front."Remember main focus and job is this ad being a straight offer ad." An AG4 ad says the offer in the first lines. An AG3 ad explains the mechanism. Everything else serves that.
  2. Proof means money."Rather use revenue number here." Then: "Stop these interest things, it's a vanity number. Say X new followers, X booked calls and X in revenue." Every client proof line is that format now. Engagement numbers can support it, never replace it.
  3. Alex's own LinkedIn is proof."Top Voice twice… a 60k+ ICP following that actually drives over 7 figures in annual revenue." It works in offer ads because it's money and it's his.
  4. Question hooks must be questions they actually ask."Nobody asks themselves that question" killed 4.2 v1. "You know why most B2B founders never grow on LinkedIn?" works because they've wondered.
  5. Every line flows on its own."This shit's gotta flow in and of itself." No stacks of fragments like "media engine… 20 brands… with 3 million…". One full sentence, one thought.
  6. Always show we do all of it."People shouldn't believe this is just us giving them access to the media engine." Even the mechanism ad says we write, publish, push and book.
  7. Never sound like a random hire."Someone else sounds low quality, could also be a random ghostwriter." It's us, a team, never "someone".
  8. Say it once, then move on."Rest accordingly to not repeat stuff for no reason." If the hook explained the problem, the body doesn't explain it again.
  9. Short beats complete."No need to put in way too much stuff, that's just gonna make it unreadable." The 4.3 how went from 55 words to 35 and got better.
  10. The new CTA."Click below and I'll show you how we'd build and run your whole LinkedIn brand completely done for you… And get you 30 qualified booked inbound calls in the next 90 days on the backend." Done-for-you is in the CTA itself now, not just in the body.
  11. Say how it feels, then fix it."And if you're starting out with a small audience… yeah… it's gonna be rough…" One honest aside before "That's why we run…" makes the mechanism feel like the answer.
  12. Put "we write it" inside the mechanism."EVERY time we publish a post we've written for our clients…" One line, and the viewer can't think we only sell the engine.
  13. Money words: dollars, every time."Over a million dollars a year", never "over a million a year". Or "a 7 figure business".
  14. End fast once the point lands.After "You shouldn't be looking for that anyways", you went straight to the offer. I'd added five lines (a "last piece", the weekly list, a done-for-you line, a second qualifier) and you cut all of them.
  15. Hand off on the word they just heard."What you should be looking for… is calls with the people who can actually buy from you. And that's exactly what we'll do for you at my company Influx." The offer picks up the exact thing just named.
  16. Offer as a statement, CTA as its own short line."We'll build and run your whole LinkedIn brand completely done for you… And get you 30 qualified booked inbound calls in the next 90 days on the backend. Click the link below and I'll show you how."
  17. Close the loop on the hook."Now sure… are you going to go viral with this process? Probably not." The ending answers the opening claim, so the ad feels finished.
  18. One mechanism per AG3 ad.3.1 is the media engine. 3.2 is the funnel. You took the media engine out of 3.2 so it didn't turn into a second 3.1.
  19. Qualify once."…for your 7 figure business" was already in the line before, so there's no extra "So if you're doing over a million dollars…" at the end.
  20. Spoken lines only."Fuck all of these instructions, how often do I have to tell you that." No [DRAW], [SHOW] or [ON SCREEN] lines in any ad. Notes and open items live outside the copy.

Line by line

My v3 on the left, your final on the right, and why yours works.

Hook

Here's what a prospect does right after a good first call with you…

Every time a prospect books a call with your B2B company…

"Every time" makes it a rule, not a story. "Your B2B company" puts the audience in line 1. Mine had no audience signal at all. And "books a call" covers every deal, not just the calls that went well.

Look-up

They look you up.

They are going to look you up…

A prediction. It sounds like someone who's watched this happen a hundred times.

Set up the cost

And if there's barely anything there… nothing that shows you actually know your stuff…

Now… Think about what's going to happen if there's barely anything there.

"Now…" resets attention. "Think about" makes them picture their own profile.

Cost 1

Then all they've got to go on is that one call. And one call usually isn't enough to hand someone a big contract.

It's going to be very hard for you to actually close them.

One line, no reasoning. They don't need the logic, they need the outcome.

Cost 2

So they go quiet… or they go with someone they've been seeing around for months. And you never really find out why.

And not only that… They'd probably even go with your competitor who HAS been active on LinkedIn.

"And not only that" stacks a worse cost on top. The CAPS land on the contrast. My last line was a dramatic closer.

The point

The fix is making sure there's something worth finding when they look.

So yes… building a LinkedIn brand really is THAT important if you want to close deals outside of your referral network.

You state the point and tie it to what they want. I skipped the point and went to a fix nobody had asked for yet.

Fix detail

Posts every week on how you think… the problems you solve… the stuff you'd tell a client. So when they look you up after that call… it backs up everything you said.

Cut.

44 words explaining what good posts are. They don't need to know. We write them.

Their excuse

Problem is… that's a lot of posting when you're running a company.

But sure… your company is doing over a million dollars a year… you're busy… There's not really any more time left in the day to write LinkedIn posts.

"But sure" agrees with them. "Over a million dollars a year" flatters and filters at the same time.

Handoff

So we just do it for you.

And that's where we step in.

Same job. Yours opens the door to an intro. Mine went straight into a list.

Intro + proof

Nothing. No intro, no result.

My name's Alex, and at my company Influx we've generated well over 8 figures for our B2B clients… Without them ever having to write a single post themselves or step in front of a camera.

Who's talking, why listen, and two objections gone, in two lines. After "no Influx" I never introduced anyone.

How

One hour with you… then we write and post every week… and our media engine gets those posts in front of the right people.

We basically write and post daily on your behalf… in your voice… for your ICP… Our media engine with over 20 brands and more than 3 million followers combined react to it within 15 minutes so it gets pushed in the algorithm…

Numbers instead of "the right people". "Basically" keeps it casual. "In your voice… for your ICP" answers "will it sound like me?" and "will it reach my buyers?"

Proof

I see it on my own calls all the time… one founder told me last week they'd been following my posts for months… And [CLIENT]… went from [X] to [X] booked calls a month…

Moved up into the intro.

Mine was 54 words: half invented detail, half placeholder. Yours is one line, said at the moment they're asking "who are you?"

Qualifier

So if you're doing over a million a year… and you're tired of good calls going quiet…

So if you're doing over a million dollars a year… And want LinkedIn to be an actual reliable acquisition channel for your business…

What they want, not what hurts. "Acquisition channel" is how an owner talks about their business, so it pulls owners.

CTA

Click below and I'll show you how we'd make sure they like what they find… Grow your LinkedIn brand…

Click below and I'll show you how we'd grow your LinkedIn brand… AND get you 30 qualified calls booked in the next 90 days on the backend.

No clever tie-back. One sentence, two layers.

My transitions that turned into a template

Mine, ad after ad

"The fix is (pretty simple)…" (2.2, 2.3) · "So let us do it" / "So we just do it for you" (2.2, 2.3) · "And look…" (1.3, 2.1) · "That's what we did for…" (2.1, 2.2) · "Which means…" (2.2) · "…the right people" (every ad I wrote)

The fix

Each is fine once. Used every time, they turned five ads into one ad. Before sending, I check every transition against the last two ads and swap any repeats.

Lingo

Owner words pull owners. Lead-gen and marketer words pull marketers and broke buyers. Vague words lose whenever a number exists.

Yours, keep

your B2B company · prospect · look you up · close them · your competitor who HAS been active · LinkedIn brand · close deals outside of your referral network · over a million dollars a year · step in · B2B clients · on your behalf · in your voice · your ICP · pushed in the algorithm · acquisition channel · qualified calls · on the backend

Mine, cut

warm leads · the right people · know your stuff · something worth finding · backs up everything you said · hand someone a big contract · go quiet · you never really find out why · make sure they like what they find · a good first call

TypeIn or outExamples
Owner and sales wordsinICP, acquisition channel, close deals, referral network, prospect, competitor
Lead-gen and creator wordsoutwarm leads, personal brand, viral, content machine, authority, impressions in hooks
Vague where a number existsout"the right people" → over 20 brands, 3M+ followers, 15 minutes
Writerly phrasesout"something worth finding", "backs up everything you said", "you never really find out why"

Style and length

CAPS: one per section, not one per ad

HAS (them against you), THAT (how much it matters), AND (the second layer of the offer). Each lands on the contrast or the stakes. My old rule said one CAPS word per ad. Yours is one per section.

Spoken grammar stays

"Our media engine with over 20 brands… react to it" isn't textbook grammar, and it doesn't matter. It's how it's said out loud. Don't correct it.

Fluff that keeps it human

basically · actually · really · probably · even. They make a claim sound like talk instead of a pitch. Your final uses each one once or twice.

Pauses were never the problem

About 20 "…" in both versions. Same rhythm on paper. The difference is what the lines say.

Length was never the problem

Every ad so far sits at 270 to 312 words, about 2 minutes. Spend them differently: less reasoning, more concrete how.

Zero made-up details

You add nothing that isn't true. I added "a busy month killed it", "first hour", "March", "this morning", "last week" and "for months", and got caught each time.

Proof

What counted, what didn't, and where it goes.

Didn't count

Scott's company page comments, 4 to 167, in an ad about closing deals: "literally zero proof". Engagement proves reach, not deals. A Tony anecdote padded with "last week" and "for months". Two proof lines stacked at the end, after the how.
