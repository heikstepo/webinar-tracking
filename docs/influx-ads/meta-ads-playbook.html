Meta ads training · What I took from it

The campaign is the funnel

A breakdown of your Meta ads training: how Meta picks which ad to show, the campaign structure that follows from it, how to write and test the ads, and the patterns in your reference scripts for three other offers. The last section maps it onto Influx.

Prepared 9 Oct 2026Sources: 6 training transcripts, 3 reference ad-script docsNext: Influx Meta Ad Map

The core idea

For high-ticket offers there is no single winning ad. There is a team of 5 to 8 ads, each with a different job, that Meta strings together into a different sequence for every prospect.

"What each campaign is, it's a funnel to get a person from the top of the funnel to the bottom of the funnel and convert. And all the ads in the campaign, which you don't want a lot of them, are not used to just compete against each other and then get the last click."Alex Becker (Hyros), quoted in the training

Ads have jobs, not just angles

Each ad does one thing in the buyer's journey: surface a problem, show an opportunity, explain the mechanism, make the offer, prove it, or handle an objection.

The words are the targeting

Targeting stays broad. Meta reads the script, visuals and speaker and finds the people that message fits. Copy written from sales-call language pulls in more people like your best buyers.

Diversity lives inside each ad

2 to 3 creatives per ad, each a different angle on the same job, plus 2 primary texts and 2 headlines. That's plenty of variety without flooding the account with ads.

How Meta decides what to show

Three mechanisms explain everything else in the training. All three come from Meta's own documentation.

Sequence learning and the purchase journey

Each time Meta can show someone an ad, it looks at their past behaviour (ads seen, content watched, what they engaged with or skipped) and asks: "Based on everything this person has done, how relevant is this ad to them right now?" Then it picks from the ads you gave it.

Meta's own example: someone who books a ski resort room next sees ads for ski equipment, lift tickets and luggage, the next step in their journey rather than more resort ads.

High-ticket buyers convert over several touches

A typical path from the training: an ad on Monday, nothing. Another on Tuesday at work, nothing. They watch your YouTube on Thursday. A third ad on Saturday gets the booking. The Saturday ad looks like the winner, but all three did the work.

The sequence is never fixed by you. Meta builds a different one for each person. A warm prospect might get the straight offer first. A cold one gets the problem ad first.

The Andromeda retrieval layer: creative is targeting

Since the Andromeda update, Meta's AI reads the ad itself (the words, the background, the type of person on screen) and matches it to people likely to respond. Speak trader language and it finds traders. Describe one exact situation and it finds people in that situation.

Consequence: the hook's job is to repel everyone who isn't the ICP. A high hook rate is meaningless for high ticket.

New ads go to warm people first

Meta shows a new ad to your warmest audience first, gathers data, then pushes it out to colder people. That's why fresh ads look great for a few days and then "fatigue". An offer with a big organic following can launch with almost anything. Once the warm pool is used up, you need a proper structure.

Why the 30-ad approach breaks

The common advice is to put 30 creatives in one ad set, all optimised for the cheapest booking. It works for a few weeks, then stops, for reasons that compound.

  1. They all chase the same people30 bottom-of-funnel ads saying "book a call" in 30 ways all compete for the same small pool of already-aware prospects.
  2. The pool shrinks and nothing refills itNo ad brings new people in, so Meta pushes the ads to colder audiences who don't respond to "book a call".
  3. That is what people call ad fatigueCost per result rises. The usual fix is to upload 30 more bottom-of-funnel ads, which drains the pool faster.
  4. Meta can't find a sequence30 ads plus text and headline variants create billions of possible sequences. Meta gives up and spends almost everything on one or two ads.
  5. You can't learn anythingWith that many variables you never know why something worked, so testing turns into hope.

Rules

  • 5 to 8 unique ads. Too many breaks sequence learning.
  • Each ad holds 2 to 3 creatives (multiple media on one ad, formerly called dynamic or flexible creative). Meta treats it as one ad with one post ID.
  • Add 2 primary texts and 2 headlines per ad. Meta finds the best combination.
  • Never mix jobs inside one ad. A problem creative, a case study and a straight offer do not belong together.
  • Use a separate campaign for each top ICP if you sell to more than one.

Why it holds up at scale

  • Top-of-funnel ads keep adding people to the aware pool, and bottom-of-funnel ads convert from it. The pool grows instead of draining.
  • That is how the training scaled to $3k to $5k a day without uploading dozens of new ads every week.
  • When something weakens, you can see which job is failing and replace just that ad.
  • Budget: spend enough to get results quickly. At $100 per booked call, $30 a day is one call every three days.

Mapping the purchase journey

You don't start by deciding "I need a top-of-funnel ad". You list the messages a prospect has to receive to go from never having heard of you to ready to buy. Each message becomes one ad. Funnel position falls out of that.

  1. Problem awarenessShow the problem with what they're doing now, in their own words.
  2. OpportunityShow the opening your approach gives them, often tied to timing (AI, a seasonal moment, a market shift).
  3. MechanismTeach how the solution works and why it beats what they tried.
  4. Straight offer"If you're X, here's what we'll do, how, and the proof."
  5. Case studyFor people who saw the offer and didn't act. Someone like them got the result.
  6. Objection handlingThe doubts that remain after the offer, taken straight from sales calls.

The references add more jobs when the market calls for it: reframe (break a false belief), competitor or alternative models (why the other options fail), borrowed authority (what a famous player in the industry did), founder proof, and timely angles like Q4.

Start from the buyer who closes

Before mapping anything, analyse sales-call transcripts and choose the ICP who actually buys, not everyone who books. In the training's Amazon example there were two: people already running a business or in a high-paying job who want a brand to exit, and people burned by other ecom models.

Writing the ads

"I don't follow frameworks when I'm writing copy." The order of decisions matters more than any template.

  1. What is this ad's job?Problem, opportunity, mechanism, offer, proof or objection.
  2. Who exactly is it for?Only the ICP who closes. Name their situation precisely.
  3. Which angle does the job?One concept per creative.
  4. Write it in their wordsUse sales-call language, knowing Meta targets based on those words.

SRPA: Situation, Reframe, Proof, Ask

  • Situation. Describe their exact situation and problem in their language. This is the callout that tells Meta who it's for.
  • Reframe. Swap the belief keeping them stuck for one that favours your solution. "You don't need better code, you need marketing."
  • Proof. Back the reframe up. "My app did $1.8M and I can't code."
  • Ask. The CTA.

Works at every stage. For top of funnel, spend longer on the situation and reframe. For bottom of funnel, spend longer explaining what they're being sent to.

The straight offer

Pitch it the way you would in person: "Are you this person? Here's the offer. Here's why you need it. Here's how it works. Here's proof. Jump in."

"If you're an app owner with over 2,000 paying users, we'll get you 500 creator videos in the next 60 days or you don't pay."Training example, a straight offer for a UGC platform
Two warnings from the training
  • Ignore hook rate and click-through rate. A hook that repels the wrong people will score badly on both, and that's fine.
  • Don't write all the ads as bottom-of-funnel ads. That rebuilds the 30-ad problem inside a 6-ad campaign.

Creative types and formats

Any type can be combined with any format. Pick the format that fits the job.

Creative types the training uses most

  • SRPA ads. Work at any stage.
  • Social proof ads. Tell a case study, then the CTA. Mostly middle and bottom of funnel.
  • Straight offer ads. The hook is the offer. Bottom of funnel.
  • Borrowed authority. Open with a famous player in the industry ("Cal AI went from nothing to..."), break down what they did, then show you sell exactly that.
  • Objection handling. Take the objections still left after the straight offer, answer one per creative, and send them back to the page.
  • Static video ads. Scannable text over B-roll, a strong bottom-of-funnel format.

Formats

  • Talking head. Clean background, camera at eye level, "like explaining it to a client across the table".
  • iPhone selfie. Walking, "like you're FaceTiming a friend". "The rawer it looks, the better it performs."
  • Physical whiteboard. Draw or reveal the mechanism step by step.
  • Mirror board. Writing on a see-through board, sometimes split screen.
  • Green screen. You in front of screenshots, stats or pages.
  • Podcast style. Mics, someone off camera asks the question, you answer naturally.
  • Split-screen case study interview. You and the client.
  • Miro-style breakdown. Walking through a case study on a canvas.
  • Static image or static video.
  • Skit. Acting out the moment, such as a failed interview.

Testing and scaling

Don't touch a working system. Let the numbers show which ad in the team is weak, then test only against that one.

  1. Launch and waitAt least 7 days, ideally around 14, so a full sales cycle passes and the campaign leaves the learning phase.
  2. Is it profitable?If yes, change nothing. Raise budget about 10% every two days.
  3. Hit the wallWhen more spend stops producing more profit, look for the weakest ad in the team.
  4. Test against itNew ad set in the same CBO campaign. One new ad with the same job, 2 to 3 new angles.
  5. Let Meta decideIf spend moves to the new ad and its cost per result is better, it replaces the old one in the control ad set.
  6. RepeatScale again until the next wall.
Metric, vs account averageTop-of-funnel ad should showBottom-of-funnel ad should showWhy

CPM▼ lower▲ higherCold, broad traffic is cheap. Aware people are a pricier audience.

A bottom-of-funnel ad with high CPM, high frequency and low spend is normal. If it also has a high cost per result, it isn't doing its job, so that's the one to test against. An ad getting almost no spend is also a weak-link signal.

Devices that recur in every script

  • Hooks are a quoted line ("I just passed my PMP!", "'Experience required.'", "'We just need better media buying'"), a contrarian claim ("Amazon private label brands are dead..."), or the exact question the buyer asks.
  • A credibility line early on: "18 years and over 700 interviews", "30 brands to 8 figures this year", "4 brand exits and a 340+ person agency".
  • A named mechanism, repeated in every creative: PM Simulation Model, Ugly Funnels, Halo Method.
  • The reframe: "You don't need X, you need Y", often with a hard "WRONG..."
  • Analogy and contrast: pilots train in simulators. The old way versus the new way, drawn on the board.
  • Named, specific stories: Sandra the nurse now on $92K remote. Biffy, hired at 52.

And in every CTA

  • A qualifier: "only for people who've spent years in another career", "if you don't have at least $X, this isn't for you", "if you're doing 100 to 500k a month". It repels the wrong people at the last second.
  • Tied to the angle: "watch this before you invest in certificates", "before you hire the next media buyer", "before you launch the next product".
  • Honest friction: "fair warning, we're expensive, but it's worth it" (Iron).
  • Spoken rhythm: short lines, ellipses for pauses, casual phrasing, and visual cues in brackets ([SHOW], [DRAW], [SCREEN]).
Lessons from the reviewer comments on the PM Revolution doc
  • Each creative needs an unmistakable audience. One comment said it wasn't sure "how Meta AI will interpret" a creative that targeted two groups at once.
  • Give the reason they're acting now, not just the outcome. Several comments asked for "the reason why they are switching".
  • Don't let one creative drift between two promises, such as the resume rewrite versus the simulator.
  • Kill angles that imply something untrue, even if they'd convert. "I wouldn't run this angle."

What it means for Influx

How the training lands on this offer, before any angles are written. The full ad map is on the Influx Meta Ad Map.

Structure

  • The current test is ABO. The training calls for one CBO campaign, one broad control ad set (US, ages 25 to 64), and 5 to 8 ads with 3 creatives each.
  • One ICP means one campaign: founder-led B2B, $1–10M. The four segments in the ICP Dossier become angles, not campaigns.
  • Influx has very little warm audience on Meta, because it sold through outbound. The "you can't mess it up" launch advantage doesn't apply. Top-of-funnel ads matter from day one.
  • Judge after 7 to 14 days. Discovery calls take a week or two to turn into a decision.

Creative

  • The callout matters even more here, because "LinkedIn" also attracts job seekers, coaches and would-be creators. Every hook should say B2B, founder, revenue band, and that they still close the deals.
  • Pick one mechanism name and repeat it everywhere, the way the references use PM Simulation Model and Halo Method. "Authority Engine" is the strongest candidate.
  • The 30-in-30 offer plays the same role as the "or you don't pay" UGC offer in the training.
  • Use the sales-call language from the ICP Dossier word for word, within the copy rules: no em dashes, no meeting claims, first-name case studies only.
